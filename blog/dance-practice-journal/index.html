How to keep a dance practice journal: what to track, with prompts

Corrections fade, small wins get forgotten, and three weeks of classes blur into one. A practice journal keeps them. What to write, a five-minute entry, prompts for when the page stays blank, and how to look back.

Ask a dancer what they worked on three weeks ago and most of us draw a blank. The corrections, the small win in Thursday's class, the section that finally clicked: they all blur together. A practice journal keeps them. It takes five minutes after you dance, and it turns a stack of classes into something you can learn from.

The short answer

A dance practice journal is a short record you write after every class or practice: the date, what you worked on, one thing that went well, one thing to fix, and a link to the video. Keep each entry to about five minutes, then read back once a week to choose what to focus on next.

  • Track: date, class or practice, piece and section, corrections you got, how your body felt, the take you filmed.
  • Write: straight after dancing, while the corrections are still in your ears.
  • Look back: weekly for patterns, monthly to see how far you've come.

Why keep a dance diary?

Because memory is a poor record of practice. You remember the class where everything went wrong and forget the five quiet ones where your turns got a little cleaner. A journal evens that out.

There's research behind it, too. A large review of 138 studies found that monitoring your progress towards a goal helps you reach it, and that the effect was larger when the progress was physically recorded rather than just kept in mind.1 Writing it down is the part that counts.

Practice research points the same way. The work behind the idea of "deliberate practice" describes improvement as effortful practice aimed at specific weaknesses, with feedback.2 A journal is where those weaknesses get named, and where the feedback from your teacher doesn't evaporate by Saturday.

The five-minute entry, step by step

  1. Write it within the hour. On the bus, in the changing room, before you shower. Corrections fade fast.
  2. Start with the facts. Date, class or practice, how long, which piece. Thirty seconds.
  3. Name one win. Be specific: not "good class" but "kept my heels down in the chassé". Small wins are the point.
  4. Name one thing to fix. Only one. A list of ten is a list of none.
  5. Copy the correction word for word. Your teacher's exact phrase is often the cue that makes it click later.
  6. Attach a take. Even 30 seconds of the section. Words say what you think happened; video shows what did. If you're not filming yet, our guide to filming yourself dancing covers the setup.
  7. Write tomorrow's first task. "Start with the arm timing in the chorus." Then you walk into the next practice with a plan.

A real entry, start to finish. "Thu 24 Oct, own practice, 40 min, Slow Burn. Win: the turn sequence in the second chorus is finally on the music. Fix: I travel too far on the slide on 6 and arrive late for 7. Teacher's note from Tuesday: 'Smaller, faster, then bigger.' Take: full run, take 3. Body: fine, calves tight. Tomorrow: slide on 6 with a mark on the floor, ten loops."

That's under a hundred words and two minutes of typing. If you practise at home between classes, pair the journal with a simple weekly practice plan, so each entry has something to report back on.

Journaling prompts for dancers

When the page stays blank, pick one prompt and answer it in two sentences.

  • After classWhich correction did I hear twice? What did the teacher demonstrate that I want to steal? What felt different today?
  • After your own practiceWhat did the video show that I didn't feel? Which eight-count still needs counting in my head?
  • After a performanceWhich moment felt most alive? What did nerves change? What would I keep exactly the same?
  • When you feel stuckWhat could I do a month ago that I can do now? (Then go and watch that month-old take.)
  • Body checkWhere am I tight, sore or tired? Is it the same place as last week?

Look back: the weekly and monthly review

A diary nobody reads is just storage. Ten minutes once a week is where the patterns show up.

  1. Read the weekAll the entries, in order. Which "thing to fix" keeps coming back?
  2. Watch two takesThe first and last take of the same section this week. Is the fix showing?
  3. Choose one focusOne skill or section for next week. Write it at the top of the next entry.

Read yesterday's entry before today's practice, too. Sleep seems to help motor skills settle: in a well-known study, people who learned a finger-tapping sequence were about 20% faster after a night's sleep, while the same hours awake brought no significant gain.3 Dance is far more complex than finger-tapping, but the habit costs nothing: start where yesterday ended, not from scratch.

Once a month, go further back. Put your first take of a piece next to the latest one. That's often the moment you see how much has changed, and it's the raw material for a dance progress video.

Paper notebook or app?

A paper notebook

Great for
Writing by hand, sketching floor patterns and formations, no screen after class.
Harder at
Connecting a note to the video it's about. Searching for "every time I worked on fouettés".

A dance diary app

Great for
Keeping the note, the take and the date together, finding a piece's history in seconds, seeing a month at a glance.
Harder at
Drawings and diagrams. Some dancers keep both: the app for takes, a notebook for formations.

A journal, not a scoreboard. Skip streaks and daily quotas; rest days belong in the diary too. If you keep noting the same pain, show your notes to your teacher or a physiotherapist. That's one of the most useful things a journal can do.

Questions, answered

What should I write in a dance journal?

The date and kind of practice, what you worked on (piece and section), one thing that went well, one thing to fix, any corrections in your teacher's own words, how your body felt, and a link to the take you filmed. Five minutes is enough.

How do I track my dance progress?

Combine short notes with video. Film the same section from the same angle regularly, write one line about what changed, and once a month put your first take next to your latest. The notes tell you what to look for; the video shows whether it happened.

How often should I write in my dance diary?

After every class or practice, ideally within an hour, and keep it short. Then read back once a week for ten minutes and choose one focus for the next week. Rest days can go in too; there's no need for a daily streak.

Is a paper dance journal or an app better?

Paper is great for handwriting and drawing formations. An app is better at keeping the note, the video and the date together and at finding a piece's history quickly. Many dancers use both.

Does keeping a practice journal actually help?

Research on goal monitoring says yes: a review of 138 studies found that tracking progress helps people reach their goals, more so when progress is physically recorded. For dancers, the journal also keeps corrections from being forgotten and makes progress visible on slow weeks.
