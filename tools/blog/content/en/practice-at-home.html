Class gives you the steps. Home is where they become yours. The trouble is that a living room has no teacher, no sprung floor and no plan, so practice either turns into scrolling for a tutorial or into the same run-through ten times. A little structure fixes most of that.

The short answer

To practise dance at home, do three to five short sessions a week of 20 to 45 minutes, each with a 10 to 15 minute warm-up, one clear focus, a few targeted drills, a run of your choreography and one recorded take. Clear a safe space, match your footwear to the floor, keep jumps low on hard floors and take at least one or two full rest days a week.

  • Floor: wood is kindest; on tile or concrete, cut the jumps. Socks or soft shoes on carpet, bare feet or grippy soles on smooth floors.
  • Session: warm up, drill, run, film, cool down.
  • Week: mix technique, choreography and conditioning, and leave room to recover.

Set up a safe space

You need less room than you think: about two by two metres for most technique, more for travelling steps and turns. What matters more is what's around and under you.

Before you start, walk the space. Move anything you could hit or trip on, tape down loose cables, and put fragile things somewhere else. The Imperial Society of Teachers of Dancing (ISTD) recommends exactly this for home dancing, and adds a good habit: walk through your choreography slowly once to find the obstacles before you dance it full out.1 Check the ceiling too, if your piece has jumps or arm lines overhead.

Floors, shoes and socks

Studios use sprung floors because they give a little under a landing, which takes some load off ankles, knees and shins. Most home floors don't give at all, so the rule is simple: the harder the floor, the fewer and lower the jumps.

Warm up properly, even for 20 minutes

At home it's tempting to skip the warm-up because there's no teacher starting the barre. Don't. A warm-up should raise your heart rate, mobilise your joints and gently stretch the muscles, gradually, so you don't overdo it before you've started.1

  1. Raise the heart rate (3–4 minutes). March or jog on the spot, bounce, step-touch to a song you like.
  2. Mobilise (3–4 minutes). Circles and rolls through the ankles, knees, hips, spine, shoulders and neck.
  3. Move dynamically (4–5 minutes). Leg swings, pliés, lunges with a twist, small grooves in your style.
  4. Rehearse the patterns (2 minutes). The movements you're about to drill, small and easy, then bigger.

Keep long held stretches mostly for after practice. In a small study with trained dancers, dynamic stretching or a combination of static and dynamic stretching gave better jump height than static stretching alone, and the combination also gave better balance.2 Ten dancers is a small study, but it matches what most teachers do: move first, hold later.

Plan each session

A good home session has one focus. "Clean the turn sequence in the second chorus" is a focus. "Practise" isn't. Here's a 30-minute shape you can stretch to 45:

Drills that work in a living room

  • Ballet and contemporaryA chair or kitchen counter as a barre: pliés, tendus, relevés and balances. Short, slow and precise beats long and sloppy.
  • Hip-hop, heels and commercialGrooves and bounces to a metronome or a single song for three minutes without stopping. Then isolations: head, chest, hips.
  • TurnsPrep and spot drills, then quarter and half turns before full ones. Socks on wood if your floor grips too much.
  • Choreography and coversLoop one eight-count until it's clean at half speed, then at tempo, then join it to the next one.
  • Partner stylesBasics and timing alone: salsa or bachata basic on the beat, frame and arm styling, footwork patterns.
  • Tap and IrishRudiments on a practice board at a slow, steady tempo, then faster in small steps.

For choreography, the way you break a piece into chunks matters as much as the time you spend. Our guide to learning choreography faster goes deeper, and counting music helps if you lose your place in the song.

Ten minutes of conditioning

Most dance injuries are in the lower body. In a systematic review of ballet injuries, the lower body (hips, legs, ankles and feet) accounted for 66 to 91 percent of all injuries across the studies.3 Strong feet, ankles and hips are your best protection, and they need no equipment:

  • Calf raises: slow, on both feet then one foot, 2 × 12.
  • Single-leg balance: 30 seconds each side, then with eyes closed.
  • Glute bridges: 2 × 12, then single-leg.
  • Side plank: 20 to 30 seconds each side.
  • Foot doming or towel scrunches: one minute each foot.

Record one take per session

The mirror shows you one angle while you're busy dancing. A recording shows you what actually happened. You don't need to film everything; one take of today's focus, from the same spot every time, is enough. Write one sentence next to it: what improved and what to fix next time.

After a few weeks those takes become something better than motivation: evidence. Keep them in a practice journal, and when you have a month or two, put the first and the latest side by side as a progress video. If you're unsure about the setup, see how to film yourself dancing.

Rest and injury prevention

Home practice comes on top of class, so it's easy to do too much. Most dance injuries come from overuse rather than accidents: in the same review, 75 percent of injuries among amateur ballet dancers were overuse injuries.3 That's the kind of injury that builds quietly from too many repetitions with too little recovery.

  • Take one or two full rest days a week. Light walking and gentle mobility are fine.
  • Vary the load. Don't put jumps or deep floor work on consecutive days.
  • Sleep counts as practice. In a well-known sleep study, people got about 20 percent faster at a newly learned finger-tapping sequence after a night's sleep, with no such gain after the same time awake. Fingers aren't feet, but the idea that skills settle overnight is a good reason not to cut sleep for extra rehearsal.4
  • Drink water during practice, not just after.

Stop and get it checked if pain is sharp, if it changes how you move, if there's swelling, or if the same spot still hurts the next morning. A physiotherapist or doctor who works with dancers can tell you what's going on and how to keep dancing safely. This guide is general information, not medical advice.
